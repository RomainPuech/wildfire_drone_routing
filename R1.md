Valuable problem framing, but the released dataset and main result have reproducibility and validity issues

Summary:
The paper is a benchmark for proactive wildfire detection built around optimization rather than a new ML method, and it comes with a semi-synthetic dataset. Given a risk map, it decides where to place ground sensors and charging stations and how to route drones. The main results are that risk-aware routing (Max-Coverage) gives the fastest detection only when the risk map is accurate, that risk-aware static placement still helps when the map is imperfect, and that drones detect fires better than ground sensors.

Contribution Type Check: Yes
Strengths:
As a benchmark/framework contribution, the paper's main value is its problem framing: it casts proactive wildfire detection as a joint decision problem that couples a risk map with sensor and charging-station placement and battery-constrained drone routing, all under a single objective of minimizing 
. The placement and rolling-horizon routing MILPs (App. E) use realistic constraints such as battery life, recharging, range, and safe return. The data comes from real, cited sources, such as Sim2Real-Fire, USFS BP, FPA-FOD, and the metrics are standard. The framework is modular. The Max-Coverage vs. Uniform-Coverage comparison also yields an finding: an inaccurate risk map can hurt risk-aware routing, though the fast-fire classes are very small (see Weaknesses).

Weaknesses:
W1: The HuggingFace repo contains only a single DroneBench.zip (2.25GB) plus README.md and .gitattributes, with no loading script, no parquet/csv, and no configs. load_dataset(repo) does not fail fast, it downloads the whole archive and returns the raw zip file rather than a structured dataset object, and the official Dataset Viewer crashes.

W2: The reported experiments are hard to reproduce from the released artifact (verified locally). The paper reports 7746 scenarios across 49 layouts, but the main experiments use only a 474 scenarios subset from 12 layouts, and the exact subset list, split, and sampling rule needed to reproduce Tables 2/3 are not released. The manifest and directory structure in the released zip are also inconsistent: the top level contains 78 layout-like folders while scenario_summary.csv lists only 49 unique layout IDs, only 63 folders contain static_risk.npy, and only about 4716/7746 scenarios appear to have fire-spread frame data.

W3: The released static_risk.npy is int16 with values of roughly 11-385, which does not match the [0,1] burn probability defined in the paper, and no normalization or unit is documented. Most fire-spread trajectories are provided as JPG mask frames rather than documented gridded numerical arrays; if these images serve as the experimental ground truth, the paper should specify the encoding, the thresholding procedure, and whether lossy JPEG compression affects the derived fire masks.

W4: inconsistency problems

Contribution claims to implement and benchmark "six wildfire monitoring strategies," but the paper lists only 5 (2 placement + 3 routing); six is therefore an error.
The abstract says open-source Python library, while the implementation is in Julia (JuMP.jl + commercial Gurobi 12.0.38), which is inconsistent.
References [14] and [15] are exact duplicates.
The claim that drone-based detection outperforms ground sensors is based on a single resource configuration: 2 drones, 8 ground sensors, and 2 charging stations. Without sensitivity analysis over fleet size, sensor count, budget, coverage radius, or deployment density, it should be read as configuration-specific rather than a general conclusion that drones are superior to ground sensors.
W5: The abstract positions the framework as a benchmark for ML researchers (assessing risk models and comparing strategies), yet all five baselines (Random, GaussianCov, MaxCov, UniCov, Brownian) are heuristic or optimization-based, with no learning-based reference, and RL appears only in future work.

W6: A fire counts as detected the moment it enters any device's sensing radius (300 m in the experiments). It models no detection probability, missed detections, false alarms, smoke or visual recognition, or sensor noise, so it does not actually exercise the ML wildfire-detection task the paper advertises.

W7: The dynamic ground-truth risk map used in Table 3 is not an independent model prediction; it is an empirical burn probability aggregated over layout and time from simulated fire-spread scenarios, and it feeds directly into the Max-Coverage objective. The paper does not state whether the scenarios used to build this map are independent of those used for evaluation, which raises a potential information leakage (train/test overlap) concern. Also, Max-Coverage's advantage over the baselines largely disappears under the independent BP risk map, with Uniform Coverage often faster on fast fires (Table 2). Was this map built from the same fire scenarios that are then evaluated? If so, would the Max-Coverage advantage still hold when the risk prediction is obtained independently?

Reproducibility: Partly
Reproducibility Comments:
Partly. The methodology is reproducible in principle — the placement and routing models are in App. E, the metrics in App. B, and an anonymized code repository is provided — but the released data artifact is not. The HuggingFace release is a single zip with no loading script or configs, so load_dataset fails and the viewer crashes, and the manifest does not match the contents (78 folders vs. 49 layout IDs in scenario_summary.csv; only 63 contain static_risk.npy; about 4716/7746 scenarios have fire-spread frames). Crucially, Tables 2/3 rely on a 474-scenario, 12-layout subset whose list, split, and sampling rule are not released, so those tables cannot be reproduced. The baselines also require the commercial Gurobi 12.0.3 solver, with no open-source fallback.

Dataset Assessment: No
Dataset Comments:
No. The dataset is downloadable, but the release is not load-dataset-compatible and lacks a published split for the 474-scenario subset used in Tables 2/3. Limitations:
The authors list several limitations: communication, terrain, and weather are abstracted away; full observability and reliable risk updates are assumed; and small fires and risk-map accuracy are left as open problems. A few points the paper does not raise but should: the 93.9% single-class imbalance, the ~6% experimental coverage (474/7746), the geometric-detection assumption, how the ground-truth risk map is built, and the continental-U.S.-only scope.

Questions:
Is the dynamic ground-truth risk map built from the test fires? Could it be reconstructed from data independent of the test fires, and Table 3 re-reported under that setting ?
Could you provide a load_dataset-compatible release, the public 474-scenario split with per-cell sample sizes n, and an open-source solver path, so that Tables 2/3 can be reproduced independently and the Fast×Big / Fast×Small conclusions checked after removing small-sample cells?
Does the framework plan to include at least one learning-based (learned/RL) baseline to support its usable by ML?
Why is detection purely geometric rather than based on a detection-probability model?
